A Worthy Liberal Counterpoint to Hazony’s Conservatism

★★★★★

I first came across Daron Acemoglu years ago through the EconTalk podcast, where I remember him making particularly sensible observations about the 2008 financial crisis. So when I came across What Happened to Liberal Democracy?, I was already familiar with his ability to explain complicated economic and institutional questions without reducing them to slogans.

My immediate reason for picking up this book, however, was Yoram Hazony's Conservatism: A Rediscovery. After reading Hazony's magnificent defence of conservatism, I felt I should read the liberal case with the same seriousness—to understand the other side rather than remain comfortably within one intellectual tradition. I was looking for a book that could make an equally compelling case for liberalism. Acemoglu's book turned out to be a worthy response to Hazony.

The book begins with the setbacks liberal democracies have experienced in recent decades and then takes a step back to ask a more fundamental question: What exactly is liberalism?

The chapter Why Liberalism is, for me, the best chapter in the book. Acemoglu's definition of liberalism is closer to the classical liberal tradition and quite distant from the socialism of Bernie Sanders or the progressivism associated with Barack Obama. He lays out the societal norms, individual responsibilities, institutional premises and fundamental pillars that make liberalism work. This chapter alone makes the book worth reading.

He then explores how ideas such as Hobbes's Social Contract and Rousseau's General Will could, paradoxically, become vehicles through which individual liberties were subsumed by an elite minority. His discussion of the state's role is particularly nuanced: the question is not whether the state should intervene, but how it can do so without allowing one section of society to dominate another.

The chapter on Liberal Success was one of the two chapters I found less captivating. It largely recounts a story that has already been told many times—the remarkable economic and social success of liberal democracies after the Second World War, and the eventual weakening of that model as industrial employment began to decline from the 1980s onward. If you want to do a deeper dive into this post-war compact and the rise and fall of good industrial jobs in America, I would recommend The End of Loyalty: The Rise and Fall of Good Jobs in America by Rick Wartzman.

Liberalism's Dilemma, however, is excellent. Acemoglu examines how liberals, going back to the tradition of John Locke, struggled to uphold liberal principles when confronted with ideas and movements they considered unacceptable, as well as with the difficult question of immigration. His discussion of education versus indoctrination is especially powerful and should be read by anyone involved in governing or shaping education systems.

In Pathway to Crisis, he explains how the post-war industrial compact gradually broke down and how the resulting prosperity became increasingly unevenly distributed. Post-Industrial Politics takes this further by examining how the "haves" and "have-nots" increasingly live in separate social worlds, making it harder for either group to understand or empathize with the other.

The chapter on Social Engineering is another masterpiece. His argument that education without critical thinking, respect for diversity and freedom of expression can become an instrument of social engineering is compelling. Even more interesting is his explanation of how administrative bureaucracies can become drivers of such engineering—not necessarily because of some grand ideological conspiracy, but because institutions and the people within them have incentives to preserve their own jobs, status and privileges. I found this argument particularly original.

Acemoglu then brings the discussion into the present with the Age of Algorithms. Algorithms and digital technologies, he argues, can deepen social divisions and accelerate the very processes of social engineering that liberal societies are struggling to contain. This creates yet another dilemma for liberalism: how do you govern technologies that can concentrate power without allowing the act of governing itself to become domination?

The final chapter, Working Class Liberalism, was the other chapter that didn't quite work for me. Once Acemoglu moves from diagnosis to prescriptions, I found the solutions considerably shallower and more familiar. The idea of "pro-worker AI", for instance, sounds appealing but strikes me as overly optimistic. In a world where billionaires and legislators are often deeply intertwined with economic interests, I find it difficult to imagine that workers' interests will simply prevail because we design a more worker-friendly technological agenda. Much of the rest of the prescription felt similarly familiar.

But these reservations don't diminish the book for me.

What I took away from What Happened to Liberal Democracy? is a much clearer understanding of what liberalism actually means—and what it does not mean. Acemoglu draws useful distinctions between classical liberalism, socialism and modern progressivism, while tackling some of liberalism's hardest questions: How tolerant can a liberal society be toward the intolerant? How do you educate without indoctrinating? How should a liberal society handle immigration while preserving the cohesion of local communities? When does state intervention become domination? And how can technology be governed without creating another form of concentrated power?

I came to this book looking for the liberal counterpart to Hazony's Conservatism: A Rediscovery. I did not agree with everything Acemoglu proposes, particularly toward the end, but I came away understanding liberalism considerably better.

Five stars—not because every argument convinced me, but because the book made me think much harder about an intellectual tradition that deserves to be understood on its own terms.
