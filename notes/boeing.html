Flying Blind

Peter Robison

A review of corporate failure, governance, regulation, and the decline of Boeing

A compelling and deeply unsettling read, Flying Blind by Peter Robison stands out as one of the most incisive examinations of corporate failure in recent times.

People, Process, and Governance

What makes this book exceptional is its clear-eyed analysis of Boeing’s decline through the lenses of people, process, and governance. Robison methodically shows how an ecosystem comprising Boeing, airlines, and the Federal Aviation Administration became so tightly interwoven that accountability was diluted. The incentives driving executives were fundamentally misaligned with passenger safety—rewarding short-term financial gains while shielding decision-makers from the consequences of long-term failures.

From Engineering to Financial Engineering

The transformation of Boeing following its merger with McDonnell Douglas is particularly striking. A company once synonymous with engineering excellence gradually succumbed to financial engineering—prioritizing stock buybacks over investment in innovation.

Robison makes a persuasive case that this shift created an environment where excessive risk-taking was not just tolerated but effectively encouraged.

Beyond Ideology

Importantly, the book avoids the trap of ideological posturing. It is not a simplistic critique of free markets; rather, it is a nuanced indictment of corporate greed and the distortion of market forces through close ties between regulators and industry.

Robison also captures how the narrative of Boeing as a national champion led to a kind of collective blind spot—where media, markets, and society amplified executive success while downplaying systemic flaws.

The Jack Welch Influence

One of the more surprising threads is the influence of Jack Welch’s philosophy, often associated with General Electric. The book reveals how the prioritization of quarterly performance over long-term resilience seeped into Boeing’s culture, with far-reaching consequences.

When Governance Fails

By the end, the reader is left with a sobering sense of unease— echoing the sentiment expressed by Jon Tester , who remarked that he would rather walk than fly on a 737 MAX.

“I would rather walk than fly on a 737 MAX.”
— Jon Tester

It’s a powerful reminder that when incentives are misaligned and governance fails, even the most respected institutions can lose their way.

★★★★★

A five-star read—thorough, balanced, and profoundly thought-provoking.
