★★★★★

Shadow City

A Book Review

Shadow City by Taran N. Khan is an unusual book about Kabul. It is part travelogue, part memoir, part cultural portrait and, almost incidentally, a history book. Most of all, it is an attempt to understand a city through the layers of memory, history and human experience embedded in it.

The book is based on Khan's visits to Kabul between 2006 and 2013, when she was working as a media professional. What makes it particularly interesting is the way she intertwines what she sees in Kabul with memories and experiences from her childhood in India. Those connections give the book a very personal quality and make Kabul feel both distant and unexpectedly familiar.

Khan is not a historian, and Shadow City does not pretend to be one. Yet I learnt a surprising amount of history from it. I had never really considered that the Pathans of India share a common ancestry with the Pashtuns of Afghanistan. I had not thought much about what happened in Afghanistan in the years after the Soviets left. And despite having read quite a few history books, I had never encountered the Hijrat Movement of 1920, when Indian Muslims opposed to the British treatment of the Ottoman Empire after the Great War migrated peacefully to Afghanistan.

The structure of the book is as interesting as its subject. It isn't a linear account of Kabul, nor is it a conventional travel book in which you constantly find yourself reaching for a map to understand where the author is. Instead, Khan travels into the mind, society and culture of Kabul.

She begins with her return to her Pashtun roots in Kabul, then moves through the lives of people who have been lost to decades of upheaval. She takes us into Kabul's literary world through its bookshops and libraries, where we experience the same delightful serendipity she does while browsing them. From there, we enter the archives of the Afghan Film Institute and encounter memories of a vanished past. The book moves through the devastating impact of opium and drugs, and eventually into something as universal—and yet as complicated in Kabul—as love.

Her writing about love is particularly beautiful:

“Love transforms. It changes the interior and exterior landscapes of our lives. The street on which the beloved lives, or is met, or is glimpsed, is different from every other. It is the path that transforms the terrain. In Urdu and Persian poetry, love is referred to as nasha, intoxication. It turns the banal into extraordinary. Nothing is as it used to be. For lovers, the city appears anew.”

The language throughout is elegant and often lyrical. One description of the demolition of a house stayed with me:

“The dust from the broken rooms rose and gathered into a massive outline against the sky. And then it collapsed, dancing away with the wind.”

And then there are the little details that make a culture come alive. I loved learning about the custom of barfi, from barf, or snow: if you brought a friend or relative the news of the first snowfall, they owed you a meal.

We discover that Afghan weddings can resemble grand Indian weddings—with one wonderfully unexpected detail: Kabul wedding halls have a place to deposit guns. And who would have imagined that, in a country so ravaged by war, the government was sufficiently concerned about extravagant wedding expenditure to introduce legislation putting a ceiling on wedding costs?

But beneath these fascinating details is a much darker story: the loss of loved ones, decades of upheaval, the crumbling of governance, and the ways in which ordinary people try to fill the gaps left by dysfunctional institutions. Faith healers, for instance, step into spaces where adequate medical care is unavailable.

The book also captures the invisibility of women with devastating economy:

“The men managed women’s lives with unquestioned (but questionable) wisdom.”

And there is, inevitably, politics. Khan's account makes it difficult not to see the contradictions in the American intervention in Afghanistan—particularly the willingness, in the name of “stability,” to work with some of the very power structures and actors whose histories were entangled with Afghanistan's civil-war years. There is a painful lesson here about the difference between governing a country and governing people. Stability imposed from above is not the same thing as legitimacy earned from those being governed. And, with a touch of irony, one is left wondering whether some of the American political class's definition of “stability” was ultimately more concerned with how things looked back on Capitol Hill than with how they felt in Kabul.

Perhaps the most striking thing about Shadow City, though, is that Kabul is never really a fixed subject. It is a city in constant flux, repeatedly remade by war, politics, migration, foreign intervention and the lives of the people who remain. The book was published in 2019, and Kabul has changed dramatically since then. The American withdrawal, the return of the Taliban and the profound changes in everyday life mean that the Kabul captured in Shadow City is already, in many ways, a historical snapshot.

Which leaves me with one selfish wish: if there is ever to be another book about Kabul—one that asks how the lives, dreams, freedoms and daily realities of its people have changed since Shadow City—I would very much root for Taran N. Khan to write it. She has already shown that she can look beyond the headlines and geopolitics and see the city as a living, breathing place inhabited by complicated human beings.

That, perhaps, is what makes Shadow City so effective. It doesn't reduce Kabul to war, terrorism, poverty or geopolitics. It allows the city to be a place of books, films, weddings, poetry, love, faith, food, memories and ordinary human lives—all existing alongside the extraordinary violence of its history.

A beautifully written and deeply humane journey into Kabul—not through its geography, but through its memory and its people. Five stars.
