★★★★★

The Mayor of Castro Street: The Life and Times of Harvey Milk

Randy Shilts has managed to do something quite remarkable with The Mayor of Castro Street: take a subject who, by his own account, was not an extraordinary personality and make an extraordinary book out of his life and times.

What I particularly enjoyed was Shilts's narrative style. He doesn't simply tell Harvey Milk's story; he takes you into Castro Street, introduces you to its characters, and gradually builds the social and political context of 1970s San Francisco. As a non-American, I found this especially valuable. The book gave me a fascinating glimpse into how city politics worked at the ground level—alliances, personalities, neighbourhood politics, elections and the peculiar relationship between local communities and centres of power.

More importantly, the book is about much more than Harvey Milk or gay rights. It gives you a window into the experience of a community that has been marginalized for much of human history. You begin to understand not just the politics of the movement, but why representation, visibility and political power mattered so profoundly to people who had spent generations being invisible.

The account of the Dan White trial is particularly striking. It shows how law and justice don't always operate in isolation from the prevailing attitudes and power structures of their time. The trial becomes an unsettling illustration of how legal interpretation can be shaped—or bent—by popular sentiment and the interests of powerful constituencies.

But perhaps my biggest takeaway was Shilts's treatment of Harvey Milk himself.

Milk isn't presented as some once-in-a-generation genius or a uniquely gifted historical figure. Shilts essentially suggests that if Harvey Milk had not existed, the movement would eventually have found someone else. The movement needed a Harvey Milk, and he happened to be the person who was in the right place at the right time.

I found that perspective remarkably refreshing.

It made me wonder how many of the "great people" we celebrate in history actually fall into this category. Perhaps history sometimes creates the need for a particular kind of person, and someone happens to be available to fill that role. The individual matters, certainly—but so do the circumstances that make that individual consequential.

And that, in a way, is where Shilts's achievement becomes most impressive. The subject may not be exceptional; the book is. Shilts makes Harvey Milk's story compelling by taking us into Castro Street, introducing us to the people around him, and patiently explaining the social, political and cultural landscape of 1970s San Francisco. By the time you understand the world in which Milk operated, you understand why his emergence mattered.

A couple of cautions

There are two things I'd keep in mind before picking this up.

First, Shilts wrote The Mayor of Castro Street before And the Band Played On. I read The Mayor of Castro Street after reading And the Band Played On, and I suspect I would have appreciated the latter even more if I had read this book first. The Mayor of Castro Street provides a lot of the historical and social context that makes the people, politics and events in And the Band Played On easier to understand.

Second, if you're not American, the first part of the book can test your patience. There is a lot of detail about 1970s San Francisco politics, local personalities and political manoeuvring, and you may occasionally wonder: Why am I reading all of this?

Stick with it.

Only after seeing the whole picture do you appreciate why Shilts spends so much time establishing the context. Those seemingly minor political details are what explain the characters, their motivations and the significance of what eventually happens.

A very good biography, therefore—but even more than that, a fascinating book about how movements, politics, personalities and historical circumstances come together to create change.
