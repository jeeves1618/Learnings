Song of Draupadi

Ira Mukhoty
★★★★★A five-star read

What happens when you retell the Mahabharata, not through the eyes of its kings, warriors and sages, but through the women who lived in their shadows? Ira Mukhoty gives us a fascinating answer.

Author
Ira Mukhoty

★★★★★   5 / 5
Themes
Women, Dharma, tradition, power and perspective

This is the Mahabharata seen through a distinctly feminine lens. And the change in perspective is not merely cosmetic. It fundamentally alters how we understand its characters, their choices, the idea of Dharma and, most importantly, the cost of the great epic's celebrated traditions.

The narrative begins well before Draupadi enters the picture. We encounter Ganga and her relationship with Shantanu, Satyavati and her expectations of becoming queen, and Amba, whose dream of marrying a man of her choice is needlessly shattered by Bhishma. We then move to Kunti, through whose eyes we understand Pandu and the circumstances surrounding their marriage. Even Gandhari, whose conventional name essentially identifies her as the princess of Gandhara, is given a personal name by Mukhoty: Gauri. It is a small but telling literary choice, allowing the woman to exist beyond her geographical identity and familial associations.

The treatment of Gandhari is another example of how Mukhoty departs from the familiar narrative. In Vyasa's Mahabharata, Gandhari's father, King Subala, initially hesitates over the marriage proposal because Dhritarashtra is blind. In Mukhoty's retelling, the episode is reframed as a political betrayal, with Gandhari discovering Dhritarashtra's blindness only at the wedding ceremony. Her decision to blindfold herself consequently acquires an entirely different emotional significance: an act of shock and silent protest. Whether or not one agrees with this interpretation, it offers an entirely new vantage point.

We eventually arrive at Draupadi, first as a young woman enjoying her childhood in Panchala, and then confronted with the horrifying prospect of marrying all five Pandava brothers.

This is one of the most interesting departures from the conventional account. In Vyasa's Sanskrit Mahabharata, Draupadi does not explicitly voice an objection when the arrangement is discussed and finalised. Mukhoty's Draupadi, however, is very much a woman of our times. She questions the proposal. She is told that Yudhishthira is the eldest and that she must accept the arrangement. Years later, when Abhimanyu is married before Yudhishthira's son, she raises the question again. Why should the rules that governed her life not apply to the next generation?

It is in these moments that the book becomes particularly powerful. When the epic is narrated through the women, the celebrated idea of Dharma begins to look considerably more fragile. Consider Draupadi's question to Yudhishthira after her humiliation in the court:

“What is this respect for tradition and culture you speak of, Husband? What kind of traditions says you must not speak ill of your elders, but you can accept their humiliation and torture of your wife?”

This question sets the tone for Mukhoty's Draupadi. She is not merely the beautiful queen, the cause of a great war or the woman whose humiliation becomes a turning point in the epic. She is a thinking, questioning individual who refuses to accept that tradition, family hierarchy or Dharma can justify the indignity inflicted upon her.

And Mukhoty does not restrict herself to the experiences of royal women. One of the most meaningful additions to the narrative is Sushila, Draupadi's maid, a fictional creation through whom the author brings attention to the invisible lives and unacknowledged sacrifices of working-class women who exist beneath the grand narratives of royalty and power. When Draupadi marries the Pandavas and leaves Panchala, Sushila must leave behind her own husband and young son, whom she will never see again, to continue serving her mistress. The royal marriage becomes a personal tragedy for someone whose name would otherwise never have appeared in the epic.

This shift in perspective is particularly evident in the way Mukhoty recounts the episode of Yudhishthira gambling away his kingdom, his brothers and eventually Draupadi. Instead of taking us straight into the royal court, Mukhoty brings us the news through Sushila. We encounter Dushasana entering Draupadi's private quarters rather than simply reading about his being sent to fetch her. The difference is subtle but powerful. We are no longer watching a grand political spectacle unfold; we are experiencing the violation of a woman's private world.

Another aspect I particularly enjoyed is the absence of supernatural explanations. Mukhoty largely grounds the narrative in human motivations, political calculations, relationships and personal choices. There are no convenient divine interventions to explain away difficult events. The characters are human beings, with human ambitions, insecurities, prejudices and failings. Making this work within a story as fantastical and larger-than-life as the Mahabharata is an achievement in itself.

One small expectation worth managing: Draupadi herself appears only around halfway through the book. The title might suggest an extended account of her life, but this is really a song of Draupadi and many other women. And that, I think, is precisely what makes the book interesting.

A word of caution, though. If you are looking for a faithful English rendering of Vyasa's Sanskrit Mahabharata, this is not the book for you. Mukhoty is not attempting a translation or even a conventional retelling. She is reimagining the epic through a contemporary feminine perspective, taking creative liberties with characters, motivations and events. For a more conventional introduction to the epic, Devdutt Pattanaik's Jaya or the Penguin Classics edition of The Mahabharata would be better choices.

But if you are willing to look at this ancient world through a different lens, Song of Draupadi is an absolute delight. It makes you revisit familiar characters and episodes, question the moral certainties we have inherited and notice the people whose stories were never considered important enough to tell.

The verdict

The five stars are not for historical or textual authenticity; they are for the fresh perspective. Mukhoty does not set out to reproduce Vyasa’s Mahabharata faithfully. Instead, she gives us an imaginative retelling that allows women, whose lives and experiences are often pushed to the margins of the epic, to occupy the centre of the story. It is this shift in perspective that makes the book so compelling.
