A book review

★★★★★

Pratap: A Defiant Newspaper

By Chander Mohan and Jyotsna Mohan

A newspaper, a nation, and the courage to resist.

Pratap: A Defiant Newspaper by Chander Mohan and Jyotsna Mohan is a wonderful, highly readable piece of Indian history—and, at the same time, a thoughtful commentary on the state of journalism today.

The book opens with the bomb blast at the Pratap office in the 1980s, immediately placing the newspaper against the dark backdrop of the Punjab insurgency. From there, the authors take us back to the young Virender, who would eventually become the fearless editor of Pratap and Vir Pratap. This journey through his life becomes a remarkably intimate way of encountering India's revolutionary history.

What I particularly enjoyed is the way the book brings figures such as Bhagat Singh, Chandrasekhar Azad, Bhagwati Charan Vohra, Durga Bhabhi and Hari Kishan Talwar alive. Their stories are not presented as a collection of familiar patriotic anecdotes, but as the lives of thinking, arguing, risking and sometimes deeply conflicted young people. The treatment of the Hindustan Socialist Republican Association and its activities in western India is especially fascinating.

Bhagat Singh towers over the book—not merely because of his bravery, but because of his intellect, political clarity and honesty. The authors are also refreshingly nuanced in their treatment of the surrounding political figures. The discussion of Gandhi's approach to Lord Irwin over Bhagat Singh, for instance, avoids the simplistic narratives that dominate much of today's social-media history. It was equally refreshing to encounter Nehru as a historical figure rather than the caricature produced by what one might call the WhatsApp University version of Indian history.

The book then moves into the horrors of Partition. The authors borrow the haunting line from Dr Jekyll and Mr Hyde—“If I am the chief of the sinners, I am the chief of the sufferers too”—to capture the complicated role of ordinary people in the violence. It is a powerful reminder that history becomes uncomfortable when we stop dividing everyone neatly into heroes and villains.

And finally comes journalism itself. The book's account of the Emergency and the famous observation that the press “crawled when it was asked to bend” is particularly relevant. The Emergency damaged the credibility of the Indian press among a generation of literate Indians, and the questions raised here about courage, independence and the relationship between journalism and political power remain remarkably contemporary.

A note on sources

One reservation, though, is important for a book that makes so many historical claims. The bibliography does not appear to correspond to the paperback edition I read, which makes it difficult to trace and independently validate some of the historical incidents and assertions. The QR code provided for the notes is also currently leading to a “Page Not Found” page. That is unfortunate because the book would benefit greatly from making its sources easier for an interested reader to follow and verify.

What makes the book special is that Pratap is not merely the subject of the story. The newspaper becomes a lens through which the authors examine revolutionary nationalism, Partition, political power, censorship and the changing relationship between journalism and its readers.

An engaging history, a tribute to fearless journalism, and a timely reminder that the first duty of a newspaper is not to power—but to truth.
