A Timeline Masquerading as History, Without Narrative or Depth

Despite its title, A Short History of the Gaza Strip is anything but short. What unfolds instead is a long, repetitive chronology of events—a compilation of news-like entries organized into chapters rather than a coherent historical narrative. Each section reads the same: an attack by one side or the other, a place name, casualty figures, and a date. Open to almost any page and you’ll find yet another similar entry, making the book feel more like an endless ledger than a work of history.

A Chronology Without Narrative

The author offers no editorial voice, perspective, or narrative interpretation—just raw incidents laid end to end. The writing is dry and detached, resembling a high-school or undergraduate history assignment where the goal is simply to include the facts rather than shape them into meaning. Sentences follow predictable patterns ("In 1953…", "By the 1950s…", "Founded in Egypt in 1928…"), giving the book a monotonous, checkbox-driven tone.

The Problem of Context

Worse, the work repeatedly falls short on context. The author states in multiple places that Ben-Gurion declared the establishment of the state of Israel in 1948 but fails to mention the decisive 1947 United Nations General Assembly vote that preceded it—an omission that distorts one of the foundational moments of the region’s history. Such gaps undermine the credibility of the work as a historical account.

Holding the Narrative Together

Rather than resolving these issues through analysis or narrative cohesion, the book leans on clumsy stitching devices: “As we saw in the previous chapter” (p.50), “As we will see in the next chapter” (p.54), and frequent numerical wrap-ups starting with “In total…”. These phrases give the impression that the narrative is being held together after the fact, not constructed with intention.

A Missed Opportunity

This is especially disappointing given the emotional weight of the subject—refugees, nationalism, war, displacement—yet the writing remains clinically distant, prioritizing facts over insight, humanity, or storytelling.

Alternatives

If you're looking for meaningful, contextual, and well-crafted histories touching on Palestine and Gaza, far better options exist:

  • Robert Fisk — Pity the Nation and The Great War for Civilisation (not solely about Palestine, but deeply human and emotionally resonant)
  • Larry Collins & Dominique Lapierre — O Jerusalem (beautifully narrative-driven)
  • Howard Sachar — A History of Israel (thorough and contextual)

Final Thoughts

I bought Anne Irfan’s A Short History of Gaza hoping for a modern, authoritative account published in our era. But the plight and history of Gaza deserve an author capable of telling a story—not just compiling a timeline.
