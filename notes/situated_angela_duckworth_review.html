Book Review · Psychology · Personal Development

Situated

Find the People and Places That Bring Out Your Best
Angela Duckworth
Author
Angela Duckworth
Central themes
Situational awareness, agency and responsibility

My biggest frustration with Situated is not necessarily what it says, but the opportunity cost of reading it.

We live in a world already overflowing with words, even more so now that large language models can generate seemingly endless amounts of text. A reader's time, however, remains finite, and a book should offer something substantial in return for that investment. Unfortunately, Situated does not.

The book revolves around three broad ideas: situational awareness, agency and responsibility. None is particularly novel, and the central arguments could easily have been condensed into a reasonably good blog post. There simply isn't enough intellectual substance, scholarly rigour or original insight to justify expanding them into a book.

Much of the narrative relies on personal experiences involving the author's family, friends and acquaintances. These anecdotes are often predictable, occasionally clichéd and, at times, surprisingly naive. Consider this passage about Oprah Winfrey:

“Oprah's other mentors aren't household names: Oprah was in the fourth grade at Wharton Elementary School in Nashville, Tennessee. Mrs. Duncan was her teacher. Twenty-five years later, the producers of Oprah's nationally syndicated talk show hatched a surprise: [Oprah] took a beat to connect the dots when she looked up at the teleprompter and read the name of her next guest: Mary Duncan.”

Was Oprah really surprised? We don't know. The episode may well be touching, but this kind of sentimentality feels out of place in a book that appears to aspire to a scholarly treatment of its subject. Unfortunately, the book is full of such moments.

There is also little consideration of the inherent limitations of relying on accounts of this kind. People who agree to speak on the record, particularly those within an author's personal circle, may have their own reasons for presenting experiences favourably or reinforcing the author's arguments. Anecdotes are valuable illustrations, but they are hardly a substitute for independent evidence or critical examination.

The book also visits familiar territory: Michael Phelps, the Serenity Prayer, Maslow's hierarchy of needs, Viktor Frankl and Virginia Woolf's idea of private space in A Room of One's Own, among others. Yet these references rarely lead to deeper analysis or a fresh interpretation. They feel more like familiar intellectual signposts than building blocks in a compelling argument. Readers looking for research, synthesis or genuinely new perspectives will find very little here.

If you have already read Viktor Frankl, Daniel Kahneman, Richard Thaler or other serious thinkers who explore human agency, circumstances and the psychology of decision-making, you are unlikely to find much additional value in this book.

Ultimately, Situated feels like an extended collection of anecdotes wrapped around three fairly straightforward ideas. It could have been considerably shorter without losing anything of consequence. In an age of abundant content, that is perhaps its biggest failing: it asks for the reader's time without offering a proportionate intellectual return.

The verdict

Situated is a book whose central ideas are more substantial in promise than in execution. Its themes are worthwhile, but the treatment feels underdeveloped, leaning too heavily on personal anecdotes and familiar references. The result is an argument that could have been made more rigorously and in considerably fewer pages.

A personal reading note
