In Rebels Against the Raj, Ramachandra Guha brings to life seven extraordinary individuals who crossed oceans and cultures to make India’s freedom—and its moral conscience—their life’s work. From Annie Besant’s Home Rule movement in Chennai to Benjamin Guy Horniman’s fearless journalism in Bombay, from Mirabehn’s devotion to Gandhi to Sarala Behn’s work for women’s empowerment in Uttarakhand, these are stories of people who chose to belong to a country that was not theirs by birth, but became theirs by conviction.

Guha has chosen his subjects wisely. Annie Besant, Horniman, Mirabehn, Philip Spratt, Samuel Stokes (Satyanand), Ralph Richard Keithahn and Sarala Behn fought British colonialism, but their ambitions extended well beyond political independence. They understood that freedom from foreign rule was only the beginning; emancipation from poverty, social prejudice and intellectual servitude would be a much longer struggle. That insight gives the book a relevance that goes well beyond the period it chronicles.

Four of these rebels continued to live in India after Independence. Guha captures something particularly admirable about their relationship with their adopted country: their loyalty did not depend on the government of the day, nor did their foreign origins make them insecure about their place in India. They could challenge the state when it betrayed its ideals and support it when it lived up to them. Their patriotism was neither inherited nor performative. It was earned through commitment. In an age when identity and national belonging are often reduced to slogans, their example is refreshing.

Philip Spratt is the most compelling of the seven. Arriving in India as a communist, he eventually became a critic of the country’s socialist direction and an independent voice through Swarajya. His argument that equality cannot sustainably precede the creation of wealth strikes at a perennial political temptation: to promise a fairer distribution of prosperity before figuring out how to produce it. His warning against the “premature sponsorship of equity in wealth at the expense of creating it” feels strikingly contemporary. Spratt’s journey is a reminder that intellectual integrity sometimes requires abandoning the very ideology that first brought you to a cause.

What distinguishes these individuals is not simply that they sympathised with India, but that they immersed themselves in its life. With the partial exception of Besant, they lived among Indians as equals and comrades, rather than as missionaries, patrons or masters. They shed privilege and adopted the rhythms, relationships and responsibilities of their chosen communities. Sarala Behn’s spontaneous “Oh Baap!” when she fell down, rather than the English exclamation she might once have used, is a delightful illustration of how thoroughly India had become part of her instincts. Cultural belonging, Guha suggests, is ultimately something deeper than a passport or a declaration of allegiance.

The lives themselves offer fascinating contrasts. Sarala Behn and Keithahn built institutions designed to outlast their founders; Mirabehn’s path became a more solitary pilgrimage. And then there are the unexpected connections that make history come alive, such as Motilal Kothari, a Gandhi devotee in London, whose friendship with Mirabehn helped make him an important force behind Richard Attenborough’s film Gandhi. These details give the book texture without distracting from its larger themes.

Guha’s greatest strength here is his restraint. He allows the lives to unfold through their choices, contradictions and consequences, trusting readers to draw their own conclusions. His admiration for these figures is unmistakable, though his ideological commentary occasionally peeks through. That is a small qualification in an otherwise thoughtful and engaging work of historical recovery.

Ultimately, Rebels Against the Raj is more than a collection of biographies. It resurrects a forgotten moral fraternity: men and women who saw India not as a country to supervise or reform from the outside, but as a civilisation to which they could commit themselves completely. Their rebellion was quiet rather than theatrical, principled rather than convenient, and human rather than ideological.

A deeply rewarding book about the freedom to choose one’s country—and the courage to remain loyal to its highest ideals, even when that means disagreeing with it.
