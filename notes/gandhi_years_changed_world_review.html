I wasn't particularly a Gandhi admirer before picking up this book. I am considerably more so now. Ramachandra Guha has produced an extraordinary, deeply researched account of Gandhi's life from his return to India from South Africa. It takes nearly a thousand pages to appreciate the man in all his complexity, but every page is worth the investment.

What fascinated me most was Gandhi's ability to learn and revise his worldview. In a sense, he was a Bayesian thinker long before the term became fashionable. In his twenties in South Africa, he held the deeply prejudiced view that Indians were superior to native Africans. In India, he became a fierce opponent of untouchability, although he remained ambivalent about caste for a considerable period. Eventually, his thinking evolved to the extent that he questioned the very legitimacy of religious scriptures that sanctioned discrimination by birth.

“If a scripture is found to sanction distinctions of high and low, caste or distinctions of color, it does not deserve the name of scripture.”

What distinguishes Gandhi from many modern politicians is that he never tried to conceal these transformations. He was remarkably open about changing his mind. He once wrote:

“I do not need to refer to my past writings to say what I believe today, because only what I believe today counts.”

There is something deeply admirable about this intellectual honesty. Gandhi did not consider changing one's mind a weakness or an embarrassment. Unfortunately, this very willingness to evolve has made him an easy punching bag for India's armchair critics and pseudo-intellectuals, who prefer to judge his earlier positions without acknowledging the journey that followed.

And then there is Gandhi the nation builder, a dimension that I suspect is often underappreciated. When communal violence erupted in Noakhali, in present-day Bangladesh, Gandhi walked through the affected villages, stayed among the victims and worked relentlessly to restore peace. He did the same in Bihar and Delhi. His efforts in Bengal prompted Lord Mountbatten to make the remarkable observation:

“In the Punjab we have 55 thousand soldiers and large-scale rioting on our hands. In Bengal our forces consist of one man, and there is no rioting.”

That one man was Gandhi. I increasingly see him as a nation builder first and a freedom fighter second. His contribution to India's independence was enormous, but his greater achievement may well have been helping hold together a country that seemed almost impossible to hold together. At a time when several political leaders argued that people belonging to different religions and castes could not coexist in one country, Gandhi steadfastly refused to accept that premise. He believed in an India that accommodated its extraordinary diversity rather than seeking to erase it. In the context of his times, that was a remarkably progressive vision.

He was, however, a man of multitudes and contradictions. He championed women's participation in public life, brought women into the national movement and helped create the political space in which Sarojini Naidu became the first Indian woman president of the Congress, decades before women led major political parties in the United States or Britain. Yet, within the family, Gandhi continued to hold rather traditional views about women's roles. He could be progressive in one dimension and deeply conservative in another.

Perhaps it was this ability to hold seemingly irreconcilable positions that made him such an extraordinary negotiator and politician. He was not a man who could be neatly categorised.

His capacity to nurture leadership was equally remarkable. Jawaharlal Nehru, Vallabhbhai Patel, Maulana Abul Kalam Azad, Sarojini Naidu, Rajendra Prasad, Jivatram Bhagwandas Kripalani and Kamaladevi Chattopadhyay were among the formidable leaders who emerged and flourished in the political ecosystem he helped create. Gandhi did not merely lead a movement; he helped create an entire generation of leaders. One is tempted to ask the obvious question: how many such leaders did Jinnah leave behind?

Of course, Gandhi was not without his deeply problematic beliefs. His obsession with celibacy and abstinence, particularly during the latter half of his life, increasingly became an irrational preoccupation. He sometimes connected personal sexual thoughts and desires to the larger moral and political problems facing India. His insistence on extending the principle of absolute non-violence to confronting Nazi and Fascist aggression during the Second World War was also, in my view, impractical and detached from the brutal realities of the time. These are not trivial shortcomings, and Guha does not require us to pretend otherwise.

But perhaps the most remarkable part of the book is its account of how Gandhi unsettled the British establishment. In 1942, Churchill and Viceroy Linlithgow appeared convinced that British rule in India could continue for another thirty years. Yet, within five years, the British were gone. The book makes you appreciate just how profoundly Gandhi challenged the arrogance, confidence and moral legitimacy of the British ruling class. He did not accomplish this through military might, but through an extraordinary combination of mass mobilisation, political negotiation, moral conviction and an understanding of the vulnerabilities of the empire.

I also cannot help feeling that Gandhi deserved the Nobel Peace Prize. He was nominated several times but never received it. The Nobel Committee's failure to recognise him, particularly in 1948, remains an unfortunate episode in the history of the prize. The geopolitical considerations surrounding the British Empire and its European allies make the omission all the more uncomfortable to reflect upon.

I started this book without being particularly fond of Gandhi. I finished it convinced that he was a genuinely unique historical figure. Not because he was right about everything, but because he understood the possibilities and limitations of his time and repeatedly attempted to do what he believed was best in the circumstances. His contradictions make him human; his achievements make him extraordinary.

Guha deserves enormous credit for presenting Gandhi without either deifying or dismissing him. He gives us the historical context, the political calculations, the personal eccentricities, the intellectual evolution and, above all, the sheer scale of the man.

Perhaps Gandhi's greatest achievement was not simply helping India win its freedom, but helping Indians believe that people so different from one another could inhabit the same nation.

A thousand pages well spent. An essential read for anyone interested in Gandhi, India's independence and, more importantly, the idea of India.

The verdict

A compelling portrait of Gandhi not as a Mahatma but extraordinary nation builder—one whose intellectual evolution, political imagination and commitment to a plural India helped shape the country we know today.
